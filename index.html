Chapter 1

A Decade of Steady Climb

For most of the 2010s, rent increases were predictable. Landlords raised prices a few percent each year, roughly keeping pace with inflation and wage growth. Renters budgeted for it, planned around it, and mostly absorbed it. Nobody was thrilled, but nobody was in crisis either. That slow and steady pattern held for nearly five straight years before everything changed.

Chapter 2

The COVID Rent Shock

When offices closed and remote work became the norm, millions of Americans suddenly realized they could live anywhere. They left expensive cities in droves and flooded into mid-size metros, beach towns, and Sun Belt suburbs. Landlords in those markets had never seen this kind of demand before. Rents did not just rise. They exploded.

Sun Belt Shock

Miami, Tampa, and Riverside saw the most dramatic rent increases of any major metros in the country. The reason was straightforward: remote workers from New York, San Francisco, and Los Angeles brought coastal salaries with them when they relocated. Local renters, earning local wages, simply could not compete.

Miami, FL
$1,734 per month in Jan 2020 vs $2,665 today — up 53.7%

On the other end of the spectrum, Austin and San Francisco saw far smaller gains. Austin overbuilt aggressively and tech industry layoffs reduced demand. San Francisco lost population as remote workers left for cheaper cities. Their rent growth barely budged compared to the Sun Belt.

Takeaway
American renters paid 37% more in 2026 than they did the month before the pandemic began.

The Surge

The U.S. national rent index rose 37 percent since January 2020. That kind of sustained increase in such a short window has no modern precedent. Miami led all metros at plus 54 percent. The spike happened faster than wages, savings, or housing policy could respond.

Location Still Matters

Sun Belt cities like Miami, Tampa, and Riverside absorbed massive waves of migration and their rents reflect that. Tech hubs like Austin and San Francisco cooled or declined after 2022 as remote work demand normalized and overbuilding added supply. Where you live is not just a lifestyle choice anymore. It is a financial one.

Income Shapes Your Options

At 60 thousand dollars per year you can afford roughly 18 of the 30 cities in this dataset. At 40 thousand, fewer than 10. The affordability calculator in Chapter 4 makes this concrete. The gap between what people earn and what landlords charge has widened to a point where geography and income are increasingly the same conversation.
